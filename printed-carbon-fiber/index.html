How strong is 3D printed continuous carbon fiber? Bead geometry, same-scale sections and a layered finite element estimate

Abstract

Desktop printers that co-extrude a continuous carbon fiber strand inside a thermoplastic bead have drawn wide attention, including a Kickstarter campaign that closed with US$4.70 million pledged [3], [4]. This note quantifies what a printed part contains and what the fiber does in bending. A 0.24 mm strand in a 0.70 × 0.24 mm bead occupies 26.9% of the bead section; at an assumed 60% carbon within the strand, carbon is 16% of the bead. At one scale, published sections show a larger Markforged strand (0.33 to 0.38 mm) with 23 to 37% carbon, measured by image analysis of printed parts and by thermogravimetric analysis of the tow. A layered two-dimensional finite element model of a 4 in (101.6 mm) PETG bar in three-point bending, at 3.1% carbon by volume, predicts a 5.9-fold stiffness increase with fiber near both faces and no gain in load to first damage (1.0×) when fiber compression strength is taken as 34% of tension. Fiber on the tension face only, with half the fiber, gives 2.1× stiffness and 1.7× load to first damage. The both-face strength result depends on the compression assumption (0.8× at 25%, 1.5× at 50%); the tension-face result stays at 1.7×. In this model, fiber placement determines whether continuous fiber increases the load to first damage. With uniform full-length fiber, natural frequencies rise roughly with the square root of the stiffness ratio; other layouts change mode shapes and must be analyzed per design. All results are model estimates pending polished sections and load-frame tests.

Highlights

  • A 0.24 mm strand is 27% of a 0.70 × 0.24 mm bead; a printed part is mostly polymer.
  • Per unit path length the bead has 1.5× the Markforged bead section, mostly polymer.
  • Fiber near both faces: 5.9× stiffness, 1.0× load to first damage at 34% compression.
  • Fiber on the tension face only: 2.1× stiffness, 1.7× load to first damage.
  • The tension-face strength gain stays at 1.7× for compression ratios of 25 to 100%.

1. Introduction

Many polymer parts are almost strong enough: they meet most requirements but fall short on stiffness or strength at one location. For years I have asked whether a single strand of carbon fiber, placed where the load runs, would supply the small increment of strength and stiffness that enables the design. I strongly believed it could.

This requires fiber placed by the design, strand by strand, rather than a mask or patch of fiber laid over a region. Each strand is a towpreg: a bundle (tow) of carbon filaments pre-impregnated with a resin whose chemistry, thermoset or thermoplastic, FibreSeek does not publish. Standard-modulus carbon fiber is linear-elastic to failure at about 1.5 to 2% strain, with no yielding [1]. It should be placed only where the load requires it.

Desktop co-extrusion printers, reported at under US$3,000 [2], allow this to be tested outside an industrial laboratory. The FibreSeeker 3 (FibreSeek) Kickstarter campaign closed on 1 January 2026 with US$4,698,825 pledged by 1,539 backers against a US$50,000 goal [3], [4], with a price of US$2,699 reported at announcement in September 2025 [2]; current pricing may differ. Its headline strength, up to 900 MPa (130 ksi) [5], is given without a load direction. Comparable published values for printed continuous carbon, such as 860 MPa [6], are tensile properties in the fiber direction. Reviewer results summarized in [4] varied by load type. These are not controlled tests.

This note answers three questions. (1) What fraction of a printed bead is carbon? (2) How does the bead compare, at the same scale, with published sections from other continuous-fiber platforms? (3) Where must fiber be placed to raise stiffness and load to first damage in bending? Fiber cost, print time and part performance all depend on where, and how much, fiber is laid.

2. Process background

In co-extrusion, the strand passes through a heated composite nozzle together with metered thermoplastic and is pressed onto the previous layer as one bead. Anisoprint [7] and, per its specification, the FibreSeeker 3 [5], [8] co-extrude the strand with thermoplastic. Markforged prints a pre-impregnated fiber filament without added polymer [9]. On published systems a cutter upstream of the nozzle cuts the strand before the path ends, so each fiber run has a minimum length set by the cutter-to-tip distance, and the strand cannot follow corners tighter than its minimum bend radius. The FibreSeeker 3 values of both are not published. On these desktop platforms fiber is laid only in planar layers. In the model of Section 3.3, walls, infill and skins are unreinforced; some platforms also lay fiber as concentric rings inside walls.

For the FibreSeeker 3 this note takes a 0.24 mm (0.0094 in) strand and a 0.70 mm (0.028 in) composite nozzle. The nozzle diameter is published [5]; the strand diameter was measured by the author with a digital caliper at several locations along the supplied strand, and the readings were consistent at 0.24 mm. This note assumes fiber layers 0.24 mm tall, equal to two 0.12 mm (0.0047 in) polymer layers. The 0.24 mm fiber layer height is the author's process assumption, not a published FibreSeeker 3 setting. It is sized so that the strand is not flattened by design; any layer-height or overfill error will flatten it.

3. Methods

3.1 Bead composition

The strand area and bead area follow from the dimensions above, with the bead idealized as a rectangle of width w and height h:

As = π d2/4 = 0.0452 mm2,   Ab = w h = 0.70 × 0.24 = 0.168 mm2,   φs = As/Ab = 0.269 (1)

The carbon fraction of the bead is φc = φs Vs, where Vs is the carbon volume fraction within the strand. FibreSeek does not publish Vs. The default, 60%, is an assumption; the model allows 30 to 85%. The upper bound, 85%, is 1,000 filaments of 7 µm in a 0.24 mm strand. It approaches the hexagonal packing limit (90.7%) and exceeds the roughly 70% that impregnated tows achieve in practice, so it is a geometric bound only. A 1,000-filament tow of 8.6 µm filaments, as measured in Markforged strand [10], would not fit in 0.24 mm. A bead with semicircular ends (stadium, 0.156 mm2) gives φs = 0.291. Fig. 1 shows the bead drawn to scale, with 7 µm filaments [1] placed on a randomly jittered hexagonal lattice at Vs.

3.2 Same-scale comparison with published sections

Polished sections of Markforged material [9] and a co-extruded Anisoprint strand [7], both published under CC BY 4.0, were cropped and rescaled to a common scale. Scales were set from scale bars measured in pixels: 40 µm = 29 px (ref. [9], Fig. 4a); 50 µm = 17.5 px (ref. [9], Fig. 6b, consistent with its 125 µm layer spacing; ±0.5 px is ±3% in scale); 1,000 µm = 1,275 px (ref. [7], Fig. 9). Modeled sections were drawn at the same scale.

3.3 Finite element model

A bar 101.6 mm (4 in) between supports, 12.7 mm (1/2 in) wide and 6.24 mm (about 1/4 in) thick (52 layers of 0.12 mm) was loaded in three-point bending with 133 N (30 lbf) spread over 5 mm at midspan. The span-to-depth ratio, 16.3, matches ASTM D790. The model is linear-elastic, two-dimensional plane stress, with 120 × 52 four-node bilinear elements (2 × 2 Gauss integration) and layers perfectly bonded. On the unreinforced bar the solver agrees with Euler-Bernoulli beam theory within 1%, so shear locking is not significant at 52 elements through the thickness. This verifies the solver; it does not validate the model against tests. An independent transformed-section beam check with Timoshenko shear gives stiffness ratios of 5.99 (fiber near both faces) and 2.08 (tension face only), against 5.9 and 2.1 from the FEA, and a hand calculation for the plain bar gives 32.8 lbf to first damage, against 32 lbf from the FEA.

Each face carries two 0.12 mm polymer skin layers, then three 0.24 mm fiber layers separated by 0.12 mm polymer layers, so fiber lies 0.24 to 1.20 mm from each face. Each fiber layer holds 15 beads of 0.70 mm across 10.5 mm, with unreinforced walls about 1.1 mm wide at both edges, and is homogenized by the rule of mixtures [1] (Table 1). The model takes each fiber layer as 15 straight runs along the full bar, each cut individually, with no overlap between beads. Three layouts were solved: no fiber; three fiber layers near each face; three fiber layers near the tension face only.

The stiffness and strength efficiencies of the fiber layer (85% and 55%) are model assumptions. As a check of the material rules, Matsuzaki et al. measured 19.5 GPa and 185 MPa in tension for 6.6% continuous carbon in PLA [11]; the same rules predict about 16 GPa and 130 MPa, 17% and 30% below measurement, so the model is conservative in tension. No compression or flexure data were checked. Matsuzaki et al. impregnated a dry tow with PLA in the nozzle, so the check covers the mixing rules but not the strand-to-polymer interface used here.

Damage is assessed per element as the axial stress σx divided by an estimated first-damage stress, in tension or compression; other stress components are not checked. Elements within 6 mm of midspan and 4 mm of the supports are excluded to avoid contact stresses. Because the bending moment varies linearly along the span, each checked element is scaled to the peak midspan moment, so the reported load corresponds to the peak moment. The load to first damage is the load at which the first element reaches 1.0. For the plain bar this gives 32 lbf against about 33 lbf from beam theory. Local crushing or kinking under the loading nose is not captured.

Printed continuous carbon is much weaker in compression than in tension. The default compression strength, 34% of tension, is the ratio of one supplier's published printed-composite values (290 MPa compression, 860 MPa tension) [6]. Those values are for a cured-thermoset strand printed in PETG and may not transfer to a strand of different resin chemistry. Transferring the allowable by strain instead of by stress (290 MPa / 64 GPa = 0.45%) gives about 48% of tension for the bead. Table 4 gives results from 25 to 100%.

4. Results

4.1 Bead composition

The strand occupies 26.9% of the bead and polymer the remaining 73.1%. At the assumed strand carbon fraction, carbon is the following share of the bead:

26.9%strand share of the bead (Eq. 1)
73.1%polymer added around the strand
~16%carbon share of the bead, at 60% carbon within the strand (assumed)

The polymer added at the nozzle is 0.1228 mm2 per unit length, or 0.051 mm of 1.75 mm feedstock filament per mm of strand laid. The polymer flanks the strand on both sides. In the idealized 0.24 mm layer the strand touches the top and bottom of the bead, so cover above and below comes from the adjacent polymer layers. Encapsulation and bonding must be confirmed by sectioning.

4.2 Comparison with published sections

Fig. 2 places the bead next to published sections at one scale. The Markforged strand measures 0.33 to 0.38 mm (0.013 to 0.015 in) [10], [13] and is described as about 1,000 carbon filaments in a polyamide matrix [9]. Image analysis of printed parts gives 22.6 to 28.7% carbon by volume [10]; thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the fiber tow gives 37.4% by volume, a value for the tow, not for the printed laminate [9]. The image-analysis values are below the 34 to 71% that 1,000 filaments of 7 to 8.8 µm would give in that strand [10], so they are best read as printed-section fractions. A higher value, 69%, is reported for printed Markforged laminates in [14]; the difference in method is not resolved here. Markforged prints the strand without added polymer, and printing flattens it into 0.125 mm (0.0049 in) layers about 0.9 mm wide, with about 11% voids measured by image analysis of polished sections in ImageJ [9]. The strand area, 0.086 to 0.113 mm2, is about equal to that bead section, so the Markforged bead is essentially all strand. The Anisoprint CCF strand, a 1.5K tow pre-impregnated with a cured thermoset resin, is 0.36 mm, with about 57% carbon within the as-supplied tow (reseller listing) [6], and is co-extruded in 0.3 mm layers with large voids around the strand [7]. The 0.24 mm strand is the smallest of the three (Table 2).

4.3 Deposition rate

Per unit path length, the 0.70 × 0.24 mm bead has 1.49 times the section of the 0.9 × 0.125 mm Markforged bead. The difference is added polymer: the Markforged bead is essentially all strand, and the FibreSeeker 3 bead is 73% polymer. Because a 0.70 mm bead covers less width than a 0.9 mm bead, filling a layer of given width takes 1.29 times the path length.

Carbon per unit path length cannot be ranked from published data. The Markforged strand carries 0.019 to 0.042 mm2 of carbon (0.086 to 0.113 mm2 at 22.6 to 37.4%), and a 0.24 mm strand carries 0.014 to 0.038 mm2 at 30 to 85% (0.027 mm2 at the assumed 60%). The ranges overlap.

The published rates are 2.39 cm³/h, a print setting on a Markforged X7 [14], and 20 cm³/h in the FibreSeeker 3 specification [5], whose basis (bead volume, strand volume or nozzle capacity) is not stated. At 0.168 mm2 the specification implies about 33 mm/s head speed. The two values were obtained differently and are not directly comparable; bead size alone accounts for a factor of 1.49.

4.4 Flexural stiffness and first damage

The bar with fiber near both faces is 3.1% carbon by volume; about 19% of the bar volume is fiber bead, placed near the outer surfaces. Results for the three layouts are given in the graphical abstract and Table 3.

Fiber near both faces. Stiffness rises 5.9-fold. The top fiber layers are in compression; the model takes fiber compression strength as 34% of tension. In this model those top layers reach their estimated first-damage stress first, at about the same load as the plain bar (1.0×). This layout increases stiffness but not the load to first damage.

Fiber on the tension face only. With half the fiber, stiffness rises 2.1-fold and the load to first damage 1.7-fold. First damage moves to the polymer on the compression face. A fourth tension-face layer raises the load to first damage from 1.7× to 1.8×, because the compression-face polymer governs.

First damage is polymer yield in the plain and tension-face bars and fiber compressive failure in the both-face bar. Equal ratios therefore do not mean equal ultimate strength. Polymer yield is a conservative first-damage event relative to ultimate load.

5. Discussion

Effect of fiber placement. Continuous fiber raises stiffness wherever it is laid. At the default compression strength it raises the load to first damage only on the tension side; fiber in compression adds cost and print time and no load to first damage. Above the default the both-face layout also gains, reaching 1.5× at 50% (Table 4); the strain-based estimate in Section 3.3 (about 48%) falls near that row. In this model, continuous fiber increases strength in the fiber direction on the tension side, and stiffness elsewhere. The bending results reported in [4] could reflect fiber in compression; the layouts of those parts are not known.

Deposition rate and production value. Shorter print time lowers part cost and lead time. At a fixed machine-hour rate, halving print time halves the machine share of part cost; fiber, polymer, labor and scrap are not reduced by it. A larger bead lays more volume per unit path length, and in the co-extruded bead the added volume is polymer. Whole-part time also depends on the fiber share of the print, on travel and on cut-and-restart overhead at every fiber run; none of these has been measured here. The figure of merit is whole-part time at equal performance. No published study reports it.

Material efficiency. Infused and autoclave-cured laminates are about 45 to 65% carbon by volume [1] (Fig. 3). The bar in Section 4.4 is 3.1% carbon by volume and gains 5.9 times the stiffness. With fiber on the tension face only, about 1.55% carbon gives 2.1 times the stiffness and 1.7 times the load to first damage (default inputs). In this model that is an incredibly low amount of added material for the stiffness and strength gained, produced without molds, vacuum bagging or curing ovens. The comparison is with the unreinforced polymer part, and fiber cost was not assessed.

Dynamic behavior. Each fiber layout, lattice and carbon density gives a part its own stiffness and mass distribution, and therefore its own modal response, which must be computed or measured for each design. With uniform full-length fiber, as in the bar of Section 4.4, the mode shapes are unchanged and the natural frequencies scale with the square root of stiffness: about 2.4× for fiber near both faces and about 1.45× for the tension face only (carbon adds about 1% to the mass). Local fiber or lattice layouts change the mode shapes as well. Anisotropy, fiber in planar layers only, and the bond between fiber and polymer add to that work.

6. Limitations

  • All results are model estimates. No specimen was printed, sectioned or tested for this note.
  • The strand diameter (0.24 mm) is a caliper measurement of the supplied strand, not a manufacturer value; the carbon fraction within the strand (default 60%) is assumed; and the 0.24 mm fiber layer height is the author's process assumption. None of the three is published by the manufacturer.
  • The FEA is linear-elastic, two-dimensional and plane stress, with perfectly bonded layers. It omits fiber microbuckling, delamination, interlaminar shear failure, voids, fiber waviness, transverse strength and interlayer (Z) strength. Only σx is checked against the allowables.
  • If the strand resin is a cured thermoset, PETG bonds to it by adhesion only; perfect bonding then overstates load transfer and compression support. Beam-theory interface shear at first damage is a few MPa, low against PETG interlayer strength, but the strand-to-PETG bond strength is unknown.
  • The compression allowable is transferred by stress from a high-fraction printed composite [6]. The both-face strength result depends on it (Table 4).
  • Thermal residual stress from the expansion mismatch between PETG and carbon is not modeled. It places the fiber in compression before load is applied, and the tension-face layout is unsymmetric, so warpage and preload are expected.
  • The efficiencies (85% stiffness, 55% strength) are assumed. The layered-bar stiffness was checked against transformed-section beam theory (Section 3.3); no mesh convergence study is reported.
  • First damage is evaluated outside the load and support regions and scaled to the peak moment. Local crushing or fiber kinking under the loading nose is not captured. First damage is not final failure; the strength ratios are point predictions under the stated allowables.
  • The model is static. Natural frequencies are estimated by scaling only; mode shapes and damping were not computed.
  • The deposition rates in Table 2 are one print setting and one specification of unstated basis; they are not a like-for-like comparison.
  • Fiber and polymer cost, machine cost and depreciation, labor, scrap and failed prints, cut-and-restart overhead per fiber run, toolpath limits (minimum strand length, minimum bend radius, end anchorage beyond the supports) and machine reliability are not covered. No cost or value conclusion should be drawn from this note.

7. Future work

  • Polished sections of printed FibreSeeker 3 bars, to measure strand diameter, flattening, encapsulation and voids against Fig. 1 and Fig. 2.
  • Measurement of carbon content within the strand by image analysis of polished sections [10], cross-checked by acid digestion (ASTM D3171) or inert-atmosphere TGA with char correction, and by tow linear density with fiber density (about 1.78 g/cm³).
  • Three-point bend tests of the three layouts in Section 3.3, using the ASTM D790 geometry as modeled (specimen length at least 125 mm), with ASTM D7264 in parallel, at least five specimens per layout, to test the predicted stiffness and first-damage ratios.
  • Fiber compression strength of the printed strand in PETG (ASTM D6641) and strand-to-PETG interfacial shear (ASTM D2344 short-beam shear or pull-out), to replace the transferred compression allowable and the perfect-bond assumption.
  • A mesh convergence study, and four-point bending (ASTM D6272) to remove the load-introduction exclusion.
  • Whole-part print time and cost per part at equal performance, against an established platform.
  • Modal analysis and vibration measurement of fiber-reinforced parts and lattices.
  • Characterization of auxetic continuous-fiber lattices.

8. Conclusions

  1. A printed continuous-fiber bead is a reinforced polymer: a 0.24 mm strand is 27% of a 0.70 × 0.24 mm bead, and carbon is about 16% at the assumed strand composition.
  2. Per unit path length the 0.70 × 0.24 mm bead has 1.49 times the section of the Markforged bead; the difference is added polymer. Carbon per unit length cannot be ranked from published data.
  3. In this model, fiber raises stiffness wherever it is laid. At the assumed compression strength (34% of tension) it raises the load to first damage only on the tension side. Fiber on the tension face alone gives 2.1× stiffness and 1.7× load to first damage with half the fiber, and that strength gain does not depend on the compression assumption.
  4. In this model, a small amount of fiber on the tension side raises the load to first damage of a part that is almost strong enough. Fiber placed in compression adds stiffness, and at the assumed compression strength no load to first damage.

Predict. Print. Test.
